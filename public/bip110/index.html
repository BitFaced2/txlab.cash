BIP‑110 wants to temporarily cap arbitrary data in Bitcoin transactions. It never reached the 55% miner support a normal lock-in needs — so at block 961,632 it enters mandatory signaling, where enforcing nodes reject any block that doesn’t signal. With almost no hashrate behind it, that turns a routine upgrade into a live test of who follows which chain — possibly a split. Grab a chair; we’re watching it block by block, straight off my node.

What the rules actually change

For roughly one year (52,416 blocks) after activation, these limits would apply, then automatically lift. The target is bulk data storage — inscriptions, large OP_RETURN payloads, and similar — not ordinary payments.

Why this one is different

Minority enforcement & the split scenario

Most soft forks activate only once a super-majority of hashrate already agrees, so the network moves as one. BIP‑110 flips that: it uses mandatory signaling (a BIP‑8 “lockinontimeout” deployment). During the mandatory period, nodes running the patch reject every block that fails to signal bit 4 — whether or not most miners agree.

With support near 2.5%, that produces a stark asymmetry. If miners keep producing non-signaling blocks, BIP‑110 nodes reject the majority chain and follow whatever tiny sliver of hashrate is signaling — a chain that could crawl at a fraction of normal speed. Everyone else continues on the majority chain. That divergence is the temporary chain-split risk every exchange advisory is warning about.

Verified on my node just now: Bitcoin Core 28.1 doesn’t recognize BIP‑110 at all — getdeploymentinfo lists only the six buried/BIP‑9 forks and raises no warning about bit 4. Unpatched Core simply treats signaling blocks as ordinary. Enforcement lives entirely in patched (e.g. Ocean-aligned) nodes, which is exactly why a minority can fork itself off.

Outcomes people are watching for: (1) miners capitulate and signal to avoid orphaned blocks, so it activates almost bloodlessly; (2) support stays negligible and BIP‑110 nodes strand themselves on a minority chain until they abandon the rule; or (3) a messy, temporary split with reorg risk around the boundary. Nobody knows which — that’s why it’s worth watching.

The argument

Two readings of the same block

For — keep Bitcoin lean

  • Treats bulk data storage (inscriptions, big OP_RETURN) as an abuse of blockspace meant for money.
  • Smaller, cheaper-to-run archives; less “spam” permanently welded to every node.
  • Explicitly temporary — one year, then the limits lift on their own.
  • Backed by Ocean and a slice of node runners who want the fee-market conversation forced now.

Against — censorship & precedent

  • If a transaction pays its fee, filtering it is censorship — the mempool isn’t a moral filter.
  • Activating with ~2.5% support via coercive signaling sets a dangerous precedent for minority rule.
  • Real chain-split and reorg risk for a change most of the network never asked for.
  • Prominent opponents (incl. Michael Saylor and much of the mining industry) call the process rushed.
